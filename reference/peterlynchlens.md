# Peter Lynch Lens — Checklist

Drawn from Lynch's well-documented public frameworks (One Up on Wall Street / Beating the Street) — apply the framework, don't invent quotes attributed to him.

## 1. Classify the company (Lynch's six categories)
- **Fast grower**: revenue/EPS growth roughly >20%/yr, small-to-mid cap, still has room to expand — highest upside, highest risk
- **Stalwart**: large, steady grower (~10-12%/yr), more defensive, less explosive
- **Slow grower**: mature, low growth, usually pays a dividend — judge mainly on yield + safety, not growth
- **Cyclical**: earnings swing with the economic/commodity cycle — timing and balance-sheet strength matter more than a single year's numbers
- **Turnaround**: currently troubled but with a credible fix in motion — highest risk, needs balance-sheet survival check
- **Asset play**: hidden value on the balance sheet (real estate, cash, subsidiaries) not reflected in the stock price

State which category the stock best fits and why — the rest of the analysis (what "good" looks like) depends heavily on this classification.

## 2. "Would Lynch understand and could you explain it to a 10-year-old?"
- Favor simple, easy-to-explain businesses. Complexity or businesses that need a lot of jargon to describe are a caution flag, not a reason for exclusion on their own.

## 3. Growth at a reasonable price — PEG ratio
- PEG = P/E ÷ EPS growth rate. Roughly ≤1 is Lynch's classic "attractively priced growth" heuristic; well above 1 suggests growth may already be priced in.
- Include dividend yield in the numerator adjustment for slow growers/stalwarts (Lynch's modified PEG: P/E ÷ (growth + yield)) when relevant.

## 4. Balance sheet check (especially for cyclicals and turnarounds)
- Debt/Equity — Lynch was wary of heavily indebted companies, since debt can turn a survivable downturn into bankruptcy
- Cash position relative to debt

## 5. Inventory and receivables vs sales growth
- If inventory or receivables are growing meaningfully faster than sales, that's a classic Lynch red flag (unsold goods piling up, or loosening credit terms to customers to fake growth)

## 6. Institutional ownership & analyst coverage
- Lower institutional ownership / analyst coverage was, for Lynch, a sign a stock might still be "undiscovered" with more room to re-rate as it gets noticed — more relevant for small/mid caps than mega caps

## 7. Insider buying
- Insiders buying with their own money is one of Lynch's favorite bullish signals; insiders don't sell for many reasons, but they only buy for one

## 8. "The story" — is the growth thesis actually still intact?
- What was the original reason to expect growth (new product, new market, expansion, turnaround plan)? Check the latest numbers against that thesis — is the story still playing out, stalling, or over (i.e., has the easy growth already happened)?

## Verdict framing
Summarize as: category classification, whether the growth story still holds up in the numbers, PEG-based cheap/fair/expensive read, balance sheet safety margin, and any inventory/receivables or ownership flags — again, framed as analysis, not a buy/sell instruction.
